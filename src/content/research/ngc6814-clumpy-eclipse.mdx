We cannot take an ordinary photograph of the tiny X-ray source beside a distant supermassive black hole. Occasionally, however, nature provides a moving screen. When orbiting gas crosses our line of sight, the changing shadow can reveal structures that no telescope can directly resolve.

XMM-Newton caught such an eclipse in the active galaxy NGC 6814 in 2016. Kang and collaborators compared it with unobscured observations from 2009 and 2021, tracking not only how bright the galaxy appeared but also how its X-ray colour changed as the source dimmed and recovered.

A single smooth cloud could not explain the full pattern. The team’s preferred picture is a swarm: a denser core containing both partly transparent and X-ray-blocking clouds, embedded in a broader and more sparsely filled region. Two layers of fast, ionized gas may be fragments stripped from the same complex absorber.

In this interpretation, most of the dramatic X-ray change during the 2016 observation came from clouds moving across our view, rather than from the central engine fading on its own. The eclipse also points to an extremely compact X-ray corona, only about ten gravitational radii across, although that estimate depends on the adopted geometry.

The absorber is likely connected to a wind launched from the inner accretion flow, but that physical link remains an interpretation rather than a direct image. The wider lesson is firm: combining brightness with X-ray colour provides an unusually sensitive way to recognize hidden absorption and map the restless environment feeding a supermassive black hole.
