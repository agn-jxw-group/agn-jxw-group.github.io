The eROSITA telescope revealed enormous arcs of soft X-ray emission rising above and below the plane of the Milky Way. Their appearance suggests a pair of bubbles, but a flat picture of the sky does not tell us how far away they are. The same arcs could, in principle, be a nearby structure only hundreds of light-years across or part of a vastly larger outburst rooted near the Galactic centre.

Liu and collaborators approached this distance puzzle through shadows. A foreground cloud of gas and dust absorbs soft X-rays, so a matching dark patch tells us that the glowing X-ray structure must lie behind that cloud. Using three-dimensional maps of Galactic dust, the team identified isolated clouds roughly 500–800 parsecs away whose projected shapes closely match shadows in the North Polar Spur and a feature they named the Lotus Petal Cloud. These matches place robust lower limits of roughly 700–850 parsecs on parts of the northern structure.

Distance alone does not prove that the bright arcs belong to one bubble. The team therefore looked at polarized radio observations in regions where the X-ray emission becomes faint. The key figure shows several narrow radio arcs lining up with the visible X-ray borders. Interpreted as sections of a shock front, these arcs connect the North Polar Spur and Lotus Petal Cloud into a continuous outer boundary.

A three-dimensional tilted cup or ellipsoid model rooted near the Galactic centre reproduces that boundary. In this picture, the northern structure is several kiloparsecs across rather than a small shell in the Solar neighbourhood. Its asymmetry may reflect how an expanding shock encounters the uneven distribution of gas in the Milky Way.

The argument is morphological: it combines the silhouettes of foreground clouds with the outline traced at X-ray and radio wavelengths. It does not by itself determine exactly what powered the event. Activity from the Milky Way’s central supermassive black hole is a plausible source, but nuclear star formation or a combination of processes remains part of the broader physical discussion.

This work shows why mapping the X-ray sky is more than cataloguing bright points. Diffuse structures can preserve the history of energy released millions of years ago, and observations at other wavelengths provide the depth and geometry that a single all-sky image cannot.
