More than roughly half of unobscured active galactic nuclei show a puzzling extra glow at low X-ray energies. This **soft X-ray excess** rises above the extension of the ordinary hard X-ray spectrum, yet its remarkably common shape has resisted a single accepted explanation.

Two leading ideas make different predictions. A layer of warm, energetic particles above the accretion disk could gently boost ultraviolet disk photons into the soft X-ray band. If so, the excess should remain closely connected to the ultraviolet light. Alternatively, hard X-rays reflected from highly ionized material near the black hole could create a smooth soft signal, in which case a closer relationship with the hard X-ray corona is expected.

Chen and collaborators tested those connections across a population of 59 unobscured type-1 AGNs observed simultaneously in ultraviolet and X-ray light. Looking across many objects reduces the risk that the changing state of one source is mistaken for a universal rule.

The decisive step was to ask which relationship survives after the other is held fixed. Across three different ways of modelling the soft excess, its association with ultraviolet emission remained strong after controlling for hard X-rays. The link with hard X-rays was much weaker after ultraviolet emission was controlled.

This is population-level evidence that a warm corona is an essential part of the soft excess and may provide most of it. The conclusion does not require reflection to vanish: a secondary reflected component can still contribute in some sources. Instead, the result breaks a long-standing tie by showing which part of the central engine the excess follows most faithfully.

Connecting the soft glow to the ultraviolet disk also gives astronomers a more coherent view of black-hole feeding. The cool disk, warm corona, and hot X-ray corona are not isolated components; energy and seed photons move between them. Measuring those links is how an unresolved point of light becomes a physical map of the accretion flow.
