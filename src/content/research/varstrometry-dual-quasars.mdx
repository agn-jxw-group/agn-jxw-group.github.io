When galaxies merge, their central supermassive black holes should move toward one another before finally coalescing. The close pairs are especially important—and especially difficult to find. At large distances, two active nuclei separated by less than a kiloparsec can blur into one point even for our sharpest telescopes.

Changing light offers another route. If two unresolved sources brighten and fade independently, their combined centre of light shifts back and forth. Astronomers call this **varstrometry**: using variability to turn a tiny positional wobble into a clue that more than one source may be hiding inside a single image.

Wang and collaborators selected five distant, radio-loud quasars with strong astrometric jitter in Gaia data. They then observed them with the high-resolution e-MERLIN radio array. A compact radio source appeared in every target. In four of the five, its radio position was significantly displaced from Gaia’s average optical position.

One possible explanation is a pair of active supermassive black holes: one member dominates the radio signal while their combined, independently flickering optical light defines a shifting centre. Using the measured offsets and a plausible range of optical brightness ratios, the team inferred projected separations that can fall below one kiloparsec—a regime that ordinary imaging often misses.

These are **candidates**, not confirmed dual black holes. Gravitational lensing, a chance superposition, a single black hole displaced from its galaxy, or radio structure associated with a jet can imitate parts of the signal. Deeper, sharper observations across several wavelengths are needed to distinguish those possibilities.

The broader result is a new way to search. Gaia’s record of changing light can identify systems worth targeting, while radio interferometry tests whether the optical and radio centres truly disagree. Together, they open a window on a stage of black-hole pairing that is otherwise nearly invisible.
