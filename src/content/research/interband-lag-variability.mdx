A quasar can brighten first in blue light and answer later in red. Astronomers use that delay as a kind of cosmic echo: different colours emerge most strongly from different parts of the accretion disk, so the wait between them can help us explore a region too small and distant to photograph directly.

But does this clock always keep the same time? Su and collaborators followed 94 active galactic nuclei with six years of observations from the Zwicky Transient Facility. They divided each light curve into observing seasons and measured the colour delays again and again.

The answer was surprisingly restless. Even with a conservative test, more than half of the AGNs showed significant changes in their measured delays from one season to another.

The delays obtained from roughly one-year seasons were also typically shorter than those measured from the full six-year light curves. The length of the observing campaign therefore changes the answer, even when the underlying object is the same.

  caption="Most points favour a longer delay when all six years are analysed together, showing that the duration of the experiment matters."

The result does not require every accretion disk to rebuild itself each year. It agrees with simulations showing that the intrinsic randomness of AGN flickering, combined with a finite observing window, can make the measured echo wander. In other words, the measurement records both the hidden system and how long we listen to it.

That distinction matters whenever a delay is translated into an accretion-disk size or used to estimate other black-hole properties. Longer, more precise time-domain surveys—including WFST and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory—will let astronomers repeat the experiment for many fainter AGNs and separate persistent structure from the statistics of changing light.
