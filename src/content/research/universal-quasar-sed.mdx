Quasars can differ in brightness by enormous amounts, yet they are powered by the same basic engine: gas releasing gravitational energy as it spirals toward a supermassive black hole. Standard thin-disk models predict that changing the black hole and its feeding rate should also change the ultraviolet colour of that disk. Earlier observations appeared to support this idea, especially at the shortest and most energetic ultraviolet wavelengths.

Those extreme-ultraviolet photons are exceptionally difficult to measure. Gas between galaxies absorbs much of their light before it reaches us, and a quasar with relatively weak extreme-ultraviolet output is less likely to enter an ultraviolet-detected sample. In these data, the detection fraction rose from about 3 per cent in the faintest luminosity group to about 32 per cent in the brightest. Comparing every detected object would therefore compare differently selected populations.

Once the detection imbalance is addressed, the four curves—representing quasars across nearly two orders of magnitude in luminosity—lie almost on top of one another down to a rest-frame wavelength of about 500 angstroms. In public language, quasars with very different wattages appear to sing the same average ultraviolet tune.

This does **not** mean that every individual quasar has an identical spectrum. It means that, once an important selection effect is controlled, the population-average spectral shape shows remarkably little dependence on luminosity.

The team also reconstructed an intrinsic average spectrum after statistically accounting for absorption by intergalactic gas. It is redder in the extreme ultraviolet than several widely used quasar templates. A model in which winds remove or cool the hottest part of the disk can reproduce the median shape, suggesting that disk winds may be widespread. That interpretation is intriguing rather than proven: the work establishes the spectral shape more directly than the mechanism behind it.

This universal average spectrum matters far beyond the accretion disk. Extreme-ultraviolet photons ionize gas around quasars and contribute to the cosmic radiation field. Measuring their true abundance changes how astronomers interpret emission lines, estimate energy budgets, and connect observed light to the physics of black-hole feeding.
