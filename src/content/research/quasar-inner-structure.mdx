The region around an active supermassive black hole is not equally open in every direction. Dusty material can block some lines of sight, while radiation escapes through other directions and lights up ionized cones on much larger scales. Those cones are a projection of a three-dimensional structure that is far too small and distant to resolve directly.

Wu and collaborators combined spatially resolved emission-line maps with infrared information about warm dust. The opening angle of an ionization cone tells us how broadly radiation escapes. The infrared spectral energy distribution provides an independent estimate of the dust covering factor—the fraction of sky, as seen from the black hole, occupied by dusty material.

Their agreement supports a geometric picture in which the extended ionized gas preserves the shadow cast by the compact dusty structure close to the nucleus. It is a little like inferring the shape of a lampshade from the light pattern on a distant wall.

This result matters for galaxy evolution because geometry controls where energy can go. If radiation escapes only through narrow channels, it interacts with a different portion of the host galaxy than if the nucleus is broadly exposed. Changes in covering factor may also trace stages in the life cycle of an active galaxy.

There is an important boundary to the claim: mapping illumination is not, by itself, proof that the black hole has pushed gas out of the galaxy or shut down star formation. We present this work as a bridge to feedback studies. It establishes the shape and orientation of the central environment; forthcoming work can then test the motion, energetics and lasting influence of the gas. That distinction helps us move from seeing where radiation reaches to demonstrating what it actually does.
